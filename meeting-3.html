8. Share And Present

This bootcamp was designed with the end in mind. That means you can now update your resume, add a link to your GitHub repository on your LinkedIn profile, and prepare to speak confidently to future employers.

To conclude the bootcamp, you will have 5 minutes to present your final project — but with a twist. Rather than a traditional show-and-tell, we will frame this as a mock job interview. More and more employers are asking candidates about their experience with AI tools, so this is great practice for the types of conversations you're likely to have in the real world.

No PowerPoint slides are allowed. Just a link to your web app and a few sentences about what you learned. Your presentation should answer the types of questions you might be asked in a job interview:

  • "Tell me about a time when you used an AI tool to solve a problem. What was the problem, and how did you approach it?"
  • "Tell me about a time when AI didn't give you the result you expected. How did you troubleshoot it?"
  • "Tell me about a time when working with AI changed how you thought about a task or project. What did you learn from that experience?"

Students who successfully present a project will receive a Certificate of Completion from the Department of Accounting and Management Information Systems. The certificate recognizes that you've built and deployed a working web application and shared it with your cohort.

8.1 Presentation Tips

Between our second meeting and our third meeting, keep improving your app and prepare to present it. You have a working app—now make it something you're proud to show off.

You'll have 2–3 minutes to share your project with the group. No slides—just share your screen and talk. Prepare the following and use this structure when you present:

What to prepare

  • Live demo: Walk through your app—sign up, use the core features, show the data persisting. Practice once or twice so it goes smoothly.
  • What you built: Show your live app (the Vercel URL), click through the main features, and explain who it's for and what problem it solves (one sentence).
  • What was hard: Pick one challenge you faced. What went wrong? How did you figure it out?
  • What you learned: Pick 2–3 concepts or tools that were new to you (e.g. frontend vs backend, databases, deploying to the web). What surprised you? Would you build something again?
  • What you'd add next (optional): If you had another week, what feature would you add?

Tips

  • Practice once before presenting. Time yourself—two minutes goes fast.
  • It's okay if your app isn't perfect. Bugs happen. Unfinished features are fine. Talk about what you learned, not just what you shipped.
  • Speak to the room, not your screen. Glance at the audience.
  • Don't apologize for things that didn't work. Describe them matter-of-factly and explain what you'd do differently.

No Q&A during presentations (to keep us on time), but feel free to chat with people afterward about their projects.

8.2 Showcase Your Work

After the bootcamp, take a few steps to make your project visible to employers and your network.

Update your resume

  • Add a Projects section (or a single project line under experience) with your app name, a one-line description, and the live URL.
  • Include technologies you used (e.g. HTML/CSS/JavaScript, Supabase, Vercel, AI-assisted development).
  • Use action verbs: "Built," "Deployed," "Designed." One or two bullets is enough.

Link GitHub to LinkedIn

  • On LinkedIn: Profile → Add profile section → Featured (or About). Add a link to your GitHub profile.
  • In your GitHub profile: add your repository to pinned repos and write a short README so visitors understand what the app does and how to try it.
  • Listing your GitHub on LinkedIn helps recruiters and hiring managers see your code and projects in one place.

Optional: Write a blog post

A short post (300–400 words) about your project is a great way to reflect and show your thinking. You can publish on LinkedIn, Medium, or your own site. Use this prompt to get a first draft, then edit so it sounds like you:

Write a short blog post (300-400 words) about [your app name], a web app I built using AI-assisted development tools. The app [brief description]. During the project, I learned about [2-3 concepts]. One challenge I faced was [describe a challenge]. Write in first person, keep it conversational, and make it appropriate for LinkedIn.

Review and edit whatever AI generates—make sure it accurately describes your experience.

8.3 You Did It—Now Keep Going

Three weeks ago, you had never deployed an app. Now you have:

  • A live web application anyone can use
  • A GitHub repository showcasing your code
  • Guidance to update your resume, link GitHub to LinkedIn, and optionally share a blog post
  • Skills that will make you more effective in any role that touches technology

This isn't the end. It's a starting point. You've learned enough to be dangerous—here's how to keep going.

Keep building

The best way to learn is to build. Each project reinforces what you know and surfaces new questions. Ideas for what to do next:

  • Expand the app you just built — Add features you didn't have time for. Fix a bug that bothered you. Improve the design or copy.
  • Build something for someone else — A club, a friend, or a personal need. Real users (even one) will push you to think about clarity and edge cases.
  • Try a different kind of app — Same tools, new domain. A mobile app (e.g. React Native with Supabase), a small API, or a browser extension.
  • Revisit the fundamentals — If something felt like magic (deployment, auth, databases), dig into one concept with a tutorial or doc. You'll debug faster next time.

For curated links, tutorials, and next steps, see Continued Learning After the Bootcamp in Resources. Keep building.
